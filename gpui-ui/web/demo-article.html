Deep learning in clinical research: a short demo review

Zotero Next demo article · written for the browser demo, not a published paper · 2026
Abstract. Deep learning now reads medical images, predicts outcomes and processes clinical text. This short review, written for the Zotero Next demo, outlines where the methods came from, where they have been tested in medicine, and which reporting standards apply when they are evaluated. Hover a numbered citation to see its reference, or a figure or table mention to see it.

1. Background

Deep learning learns layered representations directly from data [1]. Convolutional networks made the first large gains in image recognition [2], and residual connections allowed much deeper networks to be trained [3]. Practical training relied on stochastic optimisers such as Adam [4] and on regularisation such as dropout [5].

Attention-based models later replaced recurrence for language tasks [6], and pre-trained transformers such as BERT [7] and large language models [8] extended them to text in general. Figure 1 sketches the size of these steps.

2. Uses in medicine

In imaging, networks have matched specialists at grading diabetic retinopathy from fundus photographs [9] and at classifying skin lesions [10], and U-Net became a standard for segmentation [11]. Interpretation methods such as SHAP explain individual predictions [12]. Broader reviews describe the convergence of human and artificial intelligence in care [13]. Table 1 lists the examples.

3. Reporting standards

Evaluations should be reported as carefully as any other clinical study: randomised trials following CONSORT [14], prediction models following TRIPOD [15], and systematic reviews following PRISMA [16]. Statistical methods such as the bootstrap [17] and the wider toolkit of statistical learning [18] remain central to honest evaluation.

4. Conclusion

The methods are mature enough to be tested in the clinic; the evidence will be as good as the trials and the reporting behind it.
